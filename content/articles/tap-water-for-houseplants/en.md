# Is tap water OK for houseplants?

For most houseplants, ordinary tap water is fine. Three things are worth knowing. Water from a softener adds salts that build up in the mix, so use unsoftened water. A few sensitive plants, such as spider plant, dracaena, prayer plant and calathea, can develop brown tips from chlorine, fluoride and other chemicals over time. And dissolved minerals from any water slowly accumulate, which is why a thorough flush every few months helps. Room-temperature water is kinder than cold. [Iowa State University Extension: brown leaf tips and edges](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/why-does-my-houseplant-have-brown-leaf-tips-and-edges)

## Softened water: avoid it

Colorado State University Extension lists salts in water, especially softened water, among the causes of salt build-up in potting mix. [Colorado State University Extension: leaching salts from potting mixes](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/houseplants/1339-leaching-salts-potting-mixes/) The University of Minnesota also specifies unsoftened water when rinsing houseplants. [University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/houseplants/spring-houseplant-care) If your home has a softener, many systems leave a kitchen tap or an outdoor tap unsoftened; use that for plants.

## Chlorine and fluoride

Some houseplants are sensitive to fluoride or chlorine in tap water. [Clemson HGIC: houseplant diseases and disorders](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/houseplant-diseases-disorders/) Iowa State names spider plant, ti plant, dracaena, prayer plant and calathea as examples that can brown at the tips over time, and suggests switching to a different water source if you suspect this. [Iowa State University Extension: brown leaf tips and edges](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/why-does-my-houseplant-have-brown-leaf-tips-and-edges)

Clemson's advice is to fill watering cans and let the water stand at room temperature for 24 hours before use. [Clemson HGIC: houseplant diseases and disorders](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/houseplant-diseases-disorders/) It is an easy habit: refill the can right after watering, and it is ready next time. We did not find an institutional source saying that standing removes fluoride, so for a sensitive plant that keeps browning, collected rainwater or filtered water is worth a try.

Brown tips have other causes too, notably dry air and uneven watering; our post on [brown leaf tips](/blog/brown-leaf-tips/) helps you rule those out first.

## Minerals build up from any water

Each time the mix dries, dissolved salts stay behind and become more concentrated. The University of Maryland recommends watering from the top until water runs out of the bottom, using at least twice the volume of the pot, and leaching like this every four to six months and before fertilising. [University of Maryland Extension: watering indoor plants](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/watering-indoor-plants)

The visible sign of build-up is a whitish crust on the mix or the rim of the pot. [Colorado State University Extension: leaching salts from potting mixes](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/houseplants/1339-leaching-salts-potting-mixes/) A soft, fuzzy layer is something else; see [white mould on houseplant soil](/blog/mold-on-houseplant-soil/).

## Temperature

Cold water can mark leaves. Iowa State notes that cold water on African violet leaves damages them quickly and causes discoloured spots, especially in winter, and recommends room-temperature water. [Iowa State University Extension: all about African violets](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/all-about-african-violets) Using water that has stood in the can overnight solves this along the way.

## Rainwater and other options

Rainwater collected in a clean container is naturally soft and suits sensitive plants, if it is not from a roof with fresh treatments. Bottled spring water often contains minerals too, so it is not automatically better. Whatever you use, the bigger factor is still how and when you water; [how to water houseplants without a rigid schedule](/guides/watering-houseplants/) covers that.

## Common questions

### Does boiled water help?

Boiling does not remove dissolved minerals, and boiled water must be fully cooled. It is not worth the effort for most plants.

### Can I use water from the dehumidifier or air conditioner?

It is low in minerals, but containers and coils can harbour dirt and microbes. If you use it, collect it in a clean container and use it fresh.

### My water is very hard. What should I do?

Flush the pots thoroughly every few months as described above, feed at or below the label rate, and repot into fresh mix when crust keeps returning. See [feeding houseplants](/guides/fertilizing-houseplants/).
