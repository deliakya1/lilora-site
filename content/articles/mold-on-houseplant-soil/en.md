# White mould on houseplant soil: is it harmful?

A white, fuzzy layer on top of the potting mix is usually a harmless fungus living on organic matter in the mix. It is unsightly rather than dangerous, but it is a useful signal: the surface is staying wet for too long, the room is humid and still, or both. Scrape it off, let the top of the mix dry between waterings, and improve airflow. A hard, white crust is a different thing: that is usually mineral salts, not mould. [Iowa State University Extension: mould on peat pots](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/will-mold-peat-pots-harm-my-flower-and-vegetable-seedlings)

## What it is

Most fungi that appear on potting mix feed on dead organic material, such as bark, wood fibre and peat, not on living roots. The University of Florida's guide to fungi in potted plants says these fungi are not necessarily harmful to plants, and that most mushroom-forming fungi are not pathogens of potted plants. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377)

The same guide lists the conditions that encourage them: high moisture, high humidity, poor drainage, high organic matter, low light and warmth. Overwatering, it notes, can encourage fungal growth. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377)

## Mould or salt crust?

The two are easy to confuse:

- **Mould** is soft and fuzzy or web-like, often appears in patches and wipes away. It usually shows up on mix that stays damp.
- **Salt crust** is hard, white to grey and crystalline. It forms on the surface of the mix and around the rim of the pot as water evaporates and leaves dissolved salts behind. [Colorado State University Extension: leaching salts from potting mixes](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/houseplants/1339-leaching-salts-potting-mixes/)

If it is crust, the answer is flushing the mix with plenty of water, not drying it out. Our post on [brown leaf tips](/blog/brown-leaf-tips/) explains how.

## What to do about mould

1. Scrape off the mouldy layer with a spoon and throw it away. If more than a thin layer is affected, replace the top 1–2 cm with fresh mix.
2. Let the top of the mix dry before you water again. Iowa State notes that mould can indicate excessive moisture and recommends letting the mix dry somewhat between waterings. [Iowa State University Extension: mould on peat pots](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/will-mold-peat-pots-harm-my-flower-and-vegetable-seedlings)
3. Give the pot more light and air. The University of Florida suggests raking the surface so it dries, and moving indoor pots to a less humid or cooler spot. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377)
4. Empty the saucer after watering and check that the drainage hole is not blocked.

Our guide to [watering houseplants without a rigid schedule](/guides/watering-houseplants/) helps you decide when the mix actually needs water.

## When it is more than cosmetic

Mould itself rarely harms the plant, but the wet conditions behind it can. If the plant wilts while the mix is wet, leaves yellow from the bottom up or the stem feels soft at the base, check the roots; see [root rot in houseplants](/guides/root-rot/). A heavy mat of fungus can also make the surface repel water, so water runs down the sides of the pot instead of soaking in. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377) In that case, replace the top layer or repot into fresh mix; [potting mix and drainage](/guides/potting-mix-and-drainage/) covers the basics.

Bright yellow, slimy blobs are usually slime moulds. The Missouri Botanical Garden notes that they are not plant parasites and disappear on their own; at most they smother small plants. [Missouri Botanical Garden: slime molds](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/insects-pests-and-problems/diseases/slime-molds)

## Little mushrooms in the pot

Small mushrooms sometimes pop up after a humid spell. Pick them out and throw them away, especially if children or pets could reach them. Touching them is not dangerous, but unknown fungi should never be eaten, and some species found in pots are poisonous. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377)

## Common questions

### Should I use a fungicide?

Usually not. Drier surface mix and better airflow solve most cases. The University of Florida advises asking your local extension service before using fungicides. [UF/IFAS: mushrooms commonly encountered in potted plants](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/PP377)

### Does cinnamon on the soil help?

It is a popular home remedy, but we did not find institutional evidence for it. Fixing moisture and airflow addresses the cause.

### Why did mould appear right after repotting?

Fresh mix is rich in organic matter and is often watered heavily after repotting. A thin bloom in the first weeks is common and usually fades as the surface dries between waterings.
