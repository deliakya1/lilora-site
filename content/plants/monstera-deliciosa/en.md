# Monstera care

Monstera is a climbing aroid native from Mexico to Panama. Indoors it needs moderate to bright light without hot direct sun, a rich draining mix that dries in its upper third before watering, warmth and some humidity. Give older plants something to climb. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to cats and dogs. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

## Light

NC State Extension describes monstera as preferring moderate brightness rather than direct sun. A position a short distance from an east or west window, or a bright room where sunbeams do not sit on the leaves, usually works. Variegated cultivars need more light than plain green ones. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

Leaves without splits on a mature plant, or long bare stems leaning toward the window, can point to too little light. See the [indoor light guide](/en/guides/indoor-plant-light-guide/) for observing a spot.

## How to check before watering

Water thoroughly, then let roughly the top quarter to a third of the mix dry before the next watering. In a 20 cm deep pot, that means checking several centimetres down, not just the surface. A wooden skewer pushed gently into the mix helps: damp particles clinging to it mean wait. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

After watering, let surplus water drain and empty any outer pot. The [watering guide](/en/guides/watering-houseplants/) explains why checks beat a fixed calendar.

## Potting mix and pot

Use a mix high in organic matter that still drains well, in a container with holes. Monstera can grow large; repot in spring if roots are crowded, moving up modestly rather than into a much larger pot. Add a moss pole or sturdy stake: in nature it climbs, and supported stems tend to produce larger leaves.

## Humidity and temperature

Monstera prefers an average warm temperature of about 16–29 °C (60–85 °F) and high humidity. In dry homes, NC State suggests a humidifier or a pebble tray. Keep it away from cold draughts and radiators. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

## Feeding

Feed during spring and summer with a balanced houseplant fertilizer at the label rate, and stop in winter. Do not feed a plant that is wilting or has wet, damaged roots.

## Seasonal care

NC State gives no separate winter schedule, so keep using the same check: water thoroughly, then wait until roughly the top quarter to third of the mix is dry. With shorter days and cooler rooms that usually takes longer, so checks come further apart. Keep the plant within its preferred 16–29 °C (60–85 °F), away from cold glass and hot air from heaters. Leave pruning and repotting for spring, as NC State suggests. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

In summer, a sunny window can become too strong; move it back if sunbeams sit on the leaves for hours. The [winter care guide](/en/guides/winter-houseplant-care/) covers draughts and heating. In Lilora, a reminder is a prompt to check, not an order to water: you record whether you watered or snoozed, so slower drying in the cold months shows up in your own history.

## Common problems

- **Yellowing lower leaves with wet mix:** check drainage and roots before watering again.
- **Brown, crisp patches:** possible sun scorch if it stands in direct sun.
- **Scale insects:** NC State lists fern scale as a common pest; check stems and leaf undersides.

The [yellow-leaf checks](/en/guides/yellow-houseplant-leaves/) help you look at the whole pattern.

## Propagation

NC State lists stem cuttings and layering. A cutting needs a node, ideally with an aerial root. Root it in water or moist mix in a warm, bright spot. Prune or repot in spring if needed. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/)

## Pets and people

The [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/cutleaf-philodendron) lists monstera (also called cutleaf philodendron) as toxic to cats and dogs because of insoluble calcium oxalates. The signs it lists are oral irritation, intense burning and irritation of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. NC State adds that the sap may irritate skin. If a pet chews it, contact a veterinarian or an animal poison control service. For people in Türkiye, the poison information line is 114 (UZEM). This is general information, not veterinary or medical advice. To compare other plants, see the [pet-safe houseplants guide](/en/guides/pet-safe-houseplants/) or the [non-toxic profiles in the plant library](/en/plants/#petNonToxic).

## Common questions

### Why doesn’t my monstera have split leaves?

Young leaves are often whole. Splits usually come with maturity, enough light and a stem that can climb.

### Is monstera a philodendron?

No. It is sometimes sold as split-leaf philodendron, but it belongs to the genus *Monstera*.

### What are the long brown roots on the stem?

Aerial roots. Guide them toward a pole or the mix; do not cut many off at once.

### Should I water monstera less in winter?

Often the gaps get longer because the mix dries more slowly. Keep checking the top quarter to third instead of switching to a new fixed calendar.
