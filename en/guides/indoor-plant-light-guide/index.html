Indoor plant light: a beginner’s guide to choosing a spot

Understand direct sunlight, bright indirect light and low light. Observe your window and choose a position around your plant’s needs.

Published by Lilora · Reviewed 2026-10-16

A short answer

Choose a position by the light reaching the leaves throughout the day. Bright indirect light means a well-lit position without direct sun striking foliage. Low light still requires usable light. Window direction is a clue; obstructions, distance and season change the result.

Understand the three labels

Direct sunlight reaches leaves as a sunbeam, including through a window. Glass does not automatically make sunlight indirect. Some houseplants suit this exposure; strong sun can damage others.

Bright indirect light describes a bright location outside direct sunbeams, or light softened by shading. Beside a sunny window or behind a sheer curtain may work, but check: a wall or dense curtain can remove more light than expected.

Low light is less intense light that some species tolerate, not a promise of growth in a dark corner. Plants need light for growth; lower light may mean slower growth or less flowering. University of Minnesota Extension

Observe the place before choosing a plant

Inspect a candidate spot in the morning, near midday and later afternoon, at the height the leaves will occupy. Record:

  1. Whether direct sun reaches the position
  2. Roughly when it arrives and leaves
  3. What blocks the window inside and outside
  4. Whether curtains normally stay open
  5. How far the foliage will sit from the glass

Repeat if the first day was unusually overcast. You are describing a place, not giving the room a permanent rating from one glance.

Light decreases away from a window. Curtains, screens, trees, buildings and glass affect what reaches the plant. A bright-looking room can contain a much darker shelf. University of Maryland Extension

Use compass directions carefully

In much of the Northern Hemisphere, an unobstructed south-facing window receives substantial direct sun; north-facing windows usually receive less. East and west often receive direct sun for part of the day. The sun-facing north/south relationship changes in the Southern Hemisphere; tropical locations need their own seasonal assessment.

A neighboring building, overhang or seasonally leafy tree may matter more than direction. Use the compass to decide where to look, then verify what reaches the foliage. Missouri Botanical Garden

Match a species to the observations

Houseplants come from different habitats. Look up the species, match its needs to the recorded conditions, and check its other requirements too.

Long, weak stems with unusually large gaps between leaves can indicate insufficient light. Bleached or scorched areas can suggest excessive exposure. These are clues, not diagnoses: water and temperature problems can overlap. University of Minnesota Extension

Make a considered adjustment and record it. Avoid repeated moves before you can observe a response.

When natural light is insufficient

A suitable grow light can support plants where daylight falls short. Choose for the species and area to illuminate. Check coverage and operating guidance; every lamp does not suit every shelf.

Intensity and duration both matter. Moving a lamp changes what reaches the leaves. Follow the maker’s placement guidance, use an appropriate timer setting and observe the response. RHS: artificial lighting

Common questions

Can a low-light plant live in a windowless room?

Only with sufficient artificial light. Low light does not mean no light. University of Minnesota Extension

Is there one correct distance from a window?

No. Window size, shading, direction, season and species all matter. Assess the actual position. University of Maryland Extension

How should I review the position later?

Photograph the plant and note when you choose the spot. Recheck as seasons, curtains or outdoor shade change. Compare new growth as well as older leaves.
