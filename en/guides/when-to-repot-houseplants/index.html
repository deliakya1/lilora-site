When to repot a houseplant, and how to do it carefully

Inspect roots, choose a sensible pot size and preserve planting depth. A careful repotting guide for common foliage houseplants.

Published by Lilora 16 October 2026 · Reviewed 16 October 2026

A short answer

Repot when roots or potting mix need attention, rather than after a fixed interval. Dense circling roots, difficult watering or degraded mix can justify repotting. Inspect first, choose a modest size increase if needed, and keep the original planting depth.

Has the plant outgrown its pot?

Roots through a drainage hole are a reason to investigate, not the whole decision. Other clues include unusually fast drying, smaller new leaves and wilting between waterings. Species differ: some need more frequent repotting; others stay in one container longer. Illinois Extension

If manageable, support the plant and slide out the root ball gently. Are roots densely circling the sides and bottom, or is there plenty of mix among them? Photograph what you find. Record the current pot size and reason for changing it. “Roots tightly circling the base” tells more than “it has been a year.”

Choose the timing and size deliberately

Active growth is a good time for routine repotting, often spring or early summer under seasonal daylight. Those months are not a worldwide rule; growth and conditions matter. Illinois Extension

Increase size modestly. For containers below 25 cm in diameter and depth, the RHS suggests roughly 2.5–5 cm wider and deeper. Consider the species and root ball instead of choosing an enormous pot to avoid future work. RHS: repotting

Sometimes refreshing worn-out mix is the useful change. Root pruning or division needs species-specific guidance, not an automatic extra step.

Prepare before removing the plant

Gather a clean draining pot, species-appropriate fresh mix, a tray and tools. Use a container mix; orchids, cacti and other specialist plants may need different media. Pre-moisten a suitable mix to help it wet evenly. Penn State Extension

For a decorative container without a hole, use a removable draining inner pot. Gravel is not a substitute for drainage: it does not give water an escape route. Iowa State University Extension

Repot a common foliage houseplant

  1. Support the root ball and ease the plant out. Squeeze a flexible nursery pot rather than pulling delicate leaves.
  2. Examine roots and gently loosen accessible circling ones. Avoid tearing through the root system; substantial cutting needs species-specific advice.
  3. Put a little fresh mix in the new pot, set in the root ball and check the height.
  4. Fill around the sides. Keep the previous growing depth, leave watering space below the rim and firm gently.
  5. For a typical healthy foliage plant, water the new mix and drain thoroughly. Specialist plants and significant root damage may require different aftercare.

Based on University of Maryland Extension and Illinois Extension.

Extensively soft or decaying roots are a root-health problem. Pause a routine pot-up; surrounding damaged roots with more wet mix will not address the cause.

Reset your observations afterward

Record the date, new pot dimensions and mix. Check moisture before the next watering; the old interval may no longer fit.

Read the mix label before feeding. Many mixes already contain fertilizer, so more may be unnecessary. Iowa State advises waiting before restarting regular feeding. Follow species needs and product guidance. Iowa State University Extension

Common questions

Does every houseplant need repotting each year?

No. Growth, species, container and mix determine the interval. Inspect rather than automatically replacing the pot. Illinois Extension

Should I remove all old mix?

Not routinely. Assess roots and medium gently and follow species-specific instructions for extensive work.

What if the plant is too heavy?

Get help and protect the workspace. Inspection should not require balancing a heavy pot or pulling hard on stems.
