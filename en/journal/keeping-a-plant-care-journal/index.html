How to keep a plant care journal (paper or app)

What to record about each plant, a simple page layout, how to read your notes after a season, and paper versus an app.

A short answer

A plant care journal is a dated list of what you saw and what you did: checked, watered, fed, moved, repotted, treated. Keep entries short and regular, add a photo now and then, and write down changes in the room as well as care. After a season you will know how often each plant really needs water, and when something goes wrong you will have a history to show instead of a guess. A notebook works as well as an app; consistency matters more than the format. Iowa State University Extension: garden journaling

Why bother writing it down

Memory is bad at intervals. "I watered it last week" is often ten days, and in a shared home it might have been someone else. Garden journals have long been recommended by extension services for exactly this reason: over time a record shows which plants thrived, which struggled, and what you did differently. Iowa State University Extension: garden journaling Penn State Extension's Master Gardeners describe the same habit, including the value of noting impressions as well as facts. Penn State Extension: keeping a garden journal

A journal also helps when a plant gets sick. Diagnosing houseplant problems depends on history: recent moves, watering, temperature changes and when symptoms started. University of Maryland Extension: diagnosing indoor plant problems The RHS suggests looking at growing conditions and recent care before treating a poorly houseplant. RHS: how to help a poorly houseplant

What to record

For each plant, once:

  • A name everyone in the home recognises, and the species if you know it
  • Where it stands: room and window
  • Pot size, pot material and whether it has a drainage hole
  • Date you got it, and a first photo

Then, as things happen:

  • Checks: the date, what the mix felt like, and whether you watered. "Checked, still moist, no water" is a real entry.
  • Watering: the date, and anything unusual such as water pooling or running straight through.
  • Feeding: date and product, so you know when to stop and start again.
  • Changes: moves, repotting, a new window, heating switched on.
  • Problems: what you saw, where on the plant, a photo, and what you did.

A simple page layout

If you use paper, one page per plant works well. Rule a few columns:

  1. Date
  2. What you saw (mix, leaves, new growth)
  3. What you did (checked only, watered, fed, moved)
  4. Initials, if several people share the care

Keep the notebook near the plants, not in a drawer. Short entries that you actually write beat detailed ones you skip.

Photos: useful, with limits

A photo every few weeks in the same spot and light shows slow changes that are hard to see day to day: a plant leaning, lower leaves yellowing, new growth. For a problem, photograph the whole plant, the affected leaves and the pot. A photo cannot show how wet the mix is, so write that down too.

How to read a journal after a season

  • Count the days between waterings in summer and in winter. The difference is your plant's real rhythm.
  • Look at what happened in the week before a problem started: a move, a missed check, a feed, a cold night.
  • Note which plants never needed much: they are often the ones that suffer from extra water.

Use those patterns to adjust your checks, not to set a rigid schedule; see how to water without a rigid schedule. When a problem appears, your notes are exactly what our guides ask you to gather: yellow houseplant leaves, overwatered or underwatered? and common houseplant pests.

Paper or app?

Paper is free, private, quick to start and pleasant to keep. Its weak points are sharing and searching: two people in different rooms cannot both see it, and finding "the last time I fed the fern" takes leafing through.

An app helps most when several people care for the same plants. Lilora is built around this kind of record: each plant has a name, a room, a photo and notes, and every check and watering is saved as its own entry with who did it and when, with an optional note. A shared household sees the same history. Whichever you choose, the habit is what counts. For households, our shared plant care guide has more.

Common questions

How often should I write something?

Whenever you check or care for a plant. A one-line entry is enough.

Should I record checks where I did not water?

Yes. They show that the plant was looked at and did not need water, which is exactly the information you lose otherwise.

What if I forget for a few weeks?

Start again. Write the date and the plant's current state; a gap is better than a guess.
