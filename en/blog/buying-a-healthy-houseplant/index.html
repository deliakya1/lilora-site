How to choose a healthy houseplant and settle it in at home

What to check before you buy a houseplant: leaves, pests, roots and pot. Then how to keep a new plant apart for two weeks and help it settle in at home.

A short answer

Most pests arrive in a home on a new plant, so a few minutes of checking in the shop pays off. Look under the leaves and at the growing tips, slide the plant out of its pot to see the roots, and pass on anything with sticky leaves, webbing, mushy roots or weeds in the pot. At home, keep the new plant away from your others for one to two weeks until you are sure it is pest-free, and give it a spot that matches its light needs. University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension: houseplant care

Before you go: know your spot

Choose for the place, not just the plant. Note how much light the spot gets, whether it is near a radiator or a draughty door, and whether cats or dogs can reach it. Our indoor plant light guide helps you judge the light, and houseplants and pets explains how to check a plant's botanical name before it comes home.

The leaves and stems

  • Hold the plant up and look at both sides of the leaves and along the stems. RHS: how to choose healthy plants
  • Avoid dead or diseased-looking leaves, grey or sooty mould, powdery mildew and broken shoots. RHS: how to choose healthy plants
  • Leaves should not be discoloured or mottled, and the plant should not be dropping them. A few small brown edges are usually acceptable. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants
  • The plant should stand firm and upright, not limp. Lanky, spindly growth is a warning sign. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants
  • Look for even, all-round growth and new shoots. RHS: how to choose healthy plants

Signs of pests

Check the undersides of leaves and the growing tips closely. University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension: houseplant care The RHS singles out scale insects and mealybugs, which suck sap and are difficult to get rid of. RHS: how to choose healthy plants The University of Minnesota adds sticky honeydew on leaves or the shelf, fine webbing, and the rims, crevices and bottoms of pots as places to look; a small magnifier helps. University of Minnesota Extension: insects on indoor plants Our guide to common houseplant pests shows what each looks like.

The pot and roots

If the shop allows it, ease the plant out of its pot:

  • Roots should be firm and white, not mushy and brown. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants
  • The roots should not be so crowded that you cannot see the compost, and roots growing out of the drainage holes suggest the plant has been in the pot too long. RHS: how to choose healthy plants
  • If the soil ball falls apart, the plant was probably moved into a bigger pot very recently. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants
  • Weeds, moss or heavy algae on the surface point to old stock. RHS: how to choose healthy plants
  • The mix should be neither bone dry nor soggy. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants

Flowers are not a sign of health in themselves, so do not choose on blooms alone. Iowa State University Extension: shopping for quality plants

The first two weeks at home

  1. Keep it apart. Isolate a new plant for one to two weeks so any pest problem has time to show. University of Minnesota Extension: insects on indoor plants A different room is best.
  2. Inspect again at the end of that period, including the undersides of leaves and the saucer.
  3. Do not repot straight away unless roots are circling badly or the pot has no drainage hole. Let it adjust to your light and temperature first; see when to repot a houseplant.
  4. Check before watering. Shop plants are often watered on a schedule; feel the mix and water only when it needs it.

Dropping a few leaves after the move is common while a plant adjusts to lower light than a greenhouse. Watch the new growth rather than worrying about one or two old leaves.

Common questions

Is it rude to take a plant out of its pot in the shop?

Ask first. Many shops are happy to let you check, or will do it for you.

Should I buy a plant in winter?

You can, but protect it from cold on the way home and expect slower growth until spring.

What if pests show up during isolation?

Keep it isolated, identify the pest, and treat it before it joins your other plants. The pests guide starts with the least harmful options.
