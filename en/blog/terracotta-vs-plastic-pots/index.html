Terracotta or plastic pots: which is better for houseplants?

Terracotta dries faster and breathes; plastic and glazed ceramic hold moisture longer. Which pot suits which plant, and why drainage matters most.

A short answer

Neither material is better for every plant; they behave differently. Unglazed terracotta is porous: air and water move through its walls, so the mix dries faster and roots get more air. That suits cacti, succulents and anyone who tends to overwater. Plastic and glazed ceramic hold moisture longer, so they suit thirsty, moisture-loving plants and people who forget to water. Whatever the material, the pot needs a drainage hole; a decorative pot without one should be used as an outer cover. Colorado State University Extension: houseplant containers

How terracotta behaves

The University of Maryland describes terra cotta pots as ones that easily absorb and lose moisture and provide excellent aeration for roots. The downsides: they dry out quickly, break easily, and their rigid walls make it harder to slide a plant out. University of Maryland Extension: potting and repotting indoor plants Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center explains why: air moves through the sides of porous clay pots, which lets the soil dry and oxygen reach the roots. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, containers

Colorado State suggests cacti, succulents, orchids and bromeliads may do better in unglazed clay. Colorado State University Extension: houseplant containers Terracotta is also heavier, which keeps tall, top-heavy plants from tipping over.

How plastic and glazed ceramic behave

Plastic pots are lightweight, inexpensive and easy to handle, and plants in them need watering less often. They can fade and become brittle in strong sun or cold outdoors. Glazed ceramic also holds moisture longer and looks good, but chips easily and often lacks enough drainage holes. University of Maryland Extension: potting and repotting indoor plants

Colorado State says moisture-loving plants grow well in plastic or ceramic, and that lightweight plastic suits large plants. Colorado State University Extension: houseplant containers Ferns, peace lilies and calatheas are typical examples.

What it means for watering

Switching material changes how often a plant needs water. Clemson notes that plants in non-porous containers such as plastic need watering less often than plants in clay. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, containers So after repotting from plastic into terracotta, check the mix more often for a few weeks, and after moving into plastic, expect longer gaps. A fixed schedule will be wrong either way; our guide to watering houseplants without a rigid schedule explains the check, and overwatered or underwatered? helps if a plant reacts.

Drainage matters more than material

Colorado State calls containers with drainage holes ideal and notes that decorative containers often have none. The solution is double potting: keep the plant in a pot with a hole and stand it inside the decorative one. Colorado State University Extension: houseplant containers Lift the inner pot out to water, or empty the outer pot after watering so the roots are never standing in water. Our guide to potting mix and drainage covers the rest, including why a layer of gravel is not a substitute for a hole.

White marks on terracotta

Over time, terracotta often develops white deposits on the outside. Colorado State notes that clay pots can show stains from salts leached from soil and fertiliser. Colorado State University Extension: houseplant containers An extension volunteer answering a reader's question suggested soaking an empty pot in a mix of three parts water to one part vinegar, then scrubbing, rinsing and drying it before reuse. Ask Extension: white fuzz on terra cotta pots If the deposits are on the mix itself, see tap water for houseplants.

Common questions

Should I soak a new terracotta pot?

Many growers do, so the dry clay does not pull water from fresh mix at first. We did not find an institutional source that requires it, but it does no harm.

Are self-watering pots a third option?

They keep a reservoir under the mix and suit thirsty plants, but they keep the lower mix constantly damp. Use them with care for plants that like to dry out.

Does pot colour matter?

Dark pots in direct sun heat up more. For most indoor spots this matters little; drainage and size matter more.
