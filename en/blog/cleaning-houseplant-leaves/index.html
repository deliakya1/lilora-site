How to clean houseplant leaves (and why it matters)

Dust blocks light that indoor plants need. How to wipe large leaves, rinse small plants, brush fuzzy leaves, and why leaf shine products are best avoided.

A short answer

Dust on leaves is more than a looks problem: it blocks some of the light a plant needs, and indoors light is already scarce. Wipe large, smooth leaves with a soft, damp cloth, rinse smaller plants under lukewarm water in the shower or sink, and use a soft brush on hairy leaves. Skip leaf shine products; extension horticulturists say they are unnecessary and can clog leaf pores. A clean every month or two, and a look at the undersides while you are there, is enough for most homes. University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care

Why dust matters

The University of Minnesota points out that dust can block light when plants need it most, at the end of a dim winter. University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center adds that clean leaves favour healthy growth and that cleaning helps control insects. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, cleaning

Cleaning is also the best moment to inspect a plant closely. Many pests start small on the undersides of leaves; our guide to common houseplant pests shows what to look for.

Large, smooth leaves

For plants such as rubber plant, monstera, fiddle-leaf fig and peace lily:

  1. Use a soft cloth dampened with lukewarm water.
  2. Support each leaf from underneath with one hand.
  3. Wipe gently from the stem towards the tip, top side first, then the underside.
  4. Change to a clean part of the cloth when it gets dirty.

The University of Minnesota recommends a gentle wipe with a damp cloth for large-leaved plants like rubber trees and monstera. University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care Clemson suggests cleaning both leaf surfaces. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, cleaning

Small or many-leaved plants

Wiping a fern or a pothos leaf by leaf is not realistic. Rinse instead: put the plant in the shower or sink and run lukewarm, unsoftened water over the foliage. University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension notes that a rinse cleans dust and insects off leaves and, done now and then, also helps flush excess salts from the mix. University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension: houseplant care

Let the pot drain fully before you put it back on its saucer, and keep the plant out of direct sun until the leaves are dry.

Hairy and fuzzy leaves

African violets and other plants with hairy leaves should not be wiped with a wet cloth. Use a soft brush, such as a clean make-up brush, to lift the dust instead. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, cleaning Cacti and succulents with a waxy or powdery coating are also best brushed rather than wiped, so the coating stays intact.

Leave the shine products on the shelf

Commercial leaf shine sprays make leaves glossy, but both sources we checked advise against them. The University of Minnesota says they are not necessary and can clog leaf pores, University of Minnesota Extension: spring houseplant care and Clemson says they are generally not recommended because the waxy residue may affect air exchange. Clemson HGIC: indoor plants, cleaning Home remedies such as milk, oil or mayonnaise leave residues too. Plain water is enough.

If you share plant care at home, a leaf clean is worth noting in your plant care journal so nobody repeats it the next day.

Common questions

How often should I clean leaves?

When you can see dust or draw a line in it with your finger. In most homes that is every month or two; near a kitchen or a busy road it may be more often.

Can I add soap to the water?

Plain water is enough for dust. Soap sprays belong to pest treatment and should be used as directed on the product, tested on a few leaves first.

Is it fine to clean leaves in winter?

Yes, and it matters most then because light is weakest. Use lukewarm water, not cold.
